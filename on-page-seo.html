01
The relevance gap

Most pages fail not on authority but on failing to say clearly what they are about

Search engines resolve a query to a page by matching intent, entities and topical completeness. Plenty of sites hold enough domain authority to rank but never do, because individual pages send scattered signals — two pages competing for one query, a title written for a brand guideline rather than a search, a heading structure that describes a layout instead of a topic. On-page SEO closes that gap, and it is usually the highest-return work available.

Keyword cannibalisation

Several pages target the same query, so engines split signals and rank none of them well. Consolidating or clearly differentiating those pages often lifts rankings without a single new backlink.

Titles written for humans only

Titles that read as brand slogans miss the query entirely. The title remains one of the strongest on-page relevance signals and the primary driver of click-through from the results page.

Thin topical coverage

A page that covers 30% of what a topic requires loses to one that covers 90%, regardless of word count. Depth means addressing the subtopics and questions the query implies, not writing longer.

Orphaned and flat link structures

Pages with few or no internal links receive little crawl priority and no authority flow. Internal linking is the cheapest ranking lever most sites leave entirely unused.

02
What on-page covers

Technical SEO makes a page crawlable. On-page makes it the right answer.

On-page SEO is everything within a page’s own content and markup that determines what query it deserves to rank for: titles, headings, body content, entity coverage, media, internal links and structured data.

It sits between technical SEO (can the page be reached and rendered?) and off-page authority (does anyone vouch for it?). On-page is the layer you fully control, which is why it usually produces the fastest measurable movement — no third-party outreach, no engineering backlog, no waiting.

  • Keyword-to-URL mapping — one primary intent per page, documented across the whole site so cannibalisation is designed out rather than discovered later.
  • Title and meta engineering — titles built to carry the query and win the click, tested against truncation limits and actual SERP competition.
  • Heading and content architecture — an H-structure that mirrors how the topic decomposes, with the subtopics and questions the query implies genuinely covered.
  • Entity and semantic coverage — the people, products, places and concepts a credible page on this topic would reference, stated explicitly rather than implied.
  • Internal link engineering — descriptive anchors, hub-and-spoke topical clusters, and authority routed deliberately toward the pages that earn revenue.
  • Structured data — the schema types your page qualifies for, validated, so you become eligible for the rich results your competitors already hold.

What is changing through 2026

Four shifts in how on-page relevance is judged

Relevance scoring has moved decisively from keyword matching to semantic and passage-level understanding. The practical implications for how you structure a page are significant.

01

Passages rank, not just pages

Engines score individual sections independently. A well-structured section can rank for a query the wider page has no claim to — which makes clear internal sectioning and self-contained subsections far more valuable than they were.

02

Entity coverage over keyword density

What matters is whether you reference the concepts, people and products a credible page on this topic would reference. Keyword frequency is close to irrelevant; semantic completeness is close to decisive.

03

Titles get rewritten by the engine

Google frequently replaces your title with its own from your H1 or body text. That makes on-page heading consistency more important, since the engine now composes what searchers see from several signals.

04

Experience signals fold into relevance

First-hand evidence — original data, real photography, named expert authorship — increasingly separates pages that rank from pages that merely cover the topic competently.

What does on-page SEO actually include?+

Everything inside a page that signals what it should rank for: title tags, meta descriptions, heading structure, body content depth and entity coverage, image optimisation and alt text, URL structure, internal links, and structured data. It excludes server-side technical work like crawl budget and rendering, and excludes off-page authority like backlinks — though all three need to work together.

How is on-page different from technical SEO?+

Technical SEO ensures a page can be crawled, rendered and indexed quickly and correctly. On-page SEO ensures that once indexed, the page is understood as the right answer to a specific query. Technical is the plumbing; on-page is the argument. A technically perfect page with unclear intent will not rank, and a well-written page that cannot be crawled will not either.

What is keyword cannibalisation and why does it matter so much?+

It is when multiple pages on your site target the same query. Engines must choose between them, signals get split, and typically none ranks as well as a single consolidated page would. It is extremely common and one of the most frequently missed causes of stalled rankings. We resolve it before any other optimisation, since strengthening two competing pages simply entrenches the conflict.

How long is the ideal page?+

There is no ideal length, and word-count targets actively cause harm. What matters is covering the subtopics and questions the query implies as completely as the best current results do, without padding. For some queries that is 400 words; for others it is 3,000. We derive the requirement from the pages currently ranking rather than applying a house rule.

Does changing my title tags risk losing rankings?+

There is short-term volatility risk, which is why we ship in controlled batches and measure per page rather than changing everything at once. Well-specified title changes usually improve both ranking and click-through. The genuine risk is unmeasured bulk edits — if you change 500 titles simultaneously with no baseline, you cannot tell what helped and what hurt.

How quickly does on-page work show results?+

Faster than most SEO work, because nothing depends on third parties. Once changes are re-crawled — usually days to a few weeks depending on your crawl frequency — ranking and CTR movement becomes visible. Depth and internal linking gains compound over two to three months as the cluster architecture takes hold.

Can you work on-page without touching our codebase?+

Usually yes. Most on-page work happens in your CMS, and we can deliver specifications your team implements, implement directly if given access, or work through a tag-management layer where necessary. Schema and internal linking sometimes need developer involvement, and we scope that explicitly rather than discovering it mid-project.

Do you rewrite our content or just advise?+

Either. Many clients take the specification and use their own writers, which works well when they have subject expertise we cannot replicate. Others have us write to specification. We are explicit that the specification is the valuable artefact — it is what makes the resulting page rank, regardless of who types it.
